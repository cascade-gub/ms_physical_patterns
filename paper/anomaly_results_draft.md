## Methods paragraph (insert after the trend-detection paragraph)

Because 23 water years is a short window for trend detection, we also asked how streamflow responds to interannual variation in its drivers without assuming a monotonic change. For every non-experimental headwater site with at least six water years meeting the discharge completeness criteria used for trend detection within 2001–2023, we converted mean daily streamflow (log-transformed), total precipitation, mean temperature and water-year MODIS GPP to within-site anomalies (z-scores). Driver anomalies were standardized over all 23 years; streamflow anomalies over the site's complete years. We summarized the within-site Spearman correlation between the streamflow anomaly and each driver across sites, including the GPP anomaly after the precipitation signal, and then the precipitation and temperature signals, had been removed from both series by regression. We then fitted linear mixed models to the pooled site-years (lme4), with fixed effects for the three driver anomalies, random slopes by site and a random intercept by water year to absorb region-wide wet and dry years, and compared sites with mean PET/P below and above 1. A log–log form of the same model gives precipitation and GPP elasticities of streamflow and, as exp(b) − 1, the proportional change in streamflow per °C. Sites admitted through the summer-window path were analysed separately on June–August flow.

## Results paragraphs (new subsection, after the streamflow-trend results)

Streamflow anomalies at the 44 non-experimental headwater sites with six or more complete water years (740 site-years; 35 humid, 9 arid) tracked precipitation almost one for one. The within-site Spearman correlation between the streamflow and precipitation anomalies had a median of 0.79 (interquartile range 0.66–0.88), was positive at every site and significant at 86 % of them (Fig. X a, b). Temperature had a weaker, opposite effect that depended on aridity: the median correlation was −0.11 at humid sites but −0.73 at arid sites, where it was negative at all nine sites and significant at seven. GPP anomalies alone showed no consistent relation to streamflow (median ρ = −0.06; 60 % of sites negative; Wilcoxon p = 0.33; Fig. X a). Removing the precipitation signal from both series left a weak negative association (median ρ = −0.09; 74 % of sites negative; p = 0.001) that was strongest at the arid sites (median −0.27), and removing temperature as well weakened it further (median ρ = −0.07; 61 % of sites negative; p = 0.03): −0.03 at humid sites (p = 0.34) and −0.21 at the nine arid sites, where it was significant at one site (Fig. X c). The pooled fits make the contrast plain: precipitation alone explains 59 % (humid) and 61 % (arid) of the variance in the streamflow anomaly (Fig. X b), whereas GPP, once precipitation and temperature are removed, explains less than 1 % of the variance that remains (Fig. X c).

The pooled mixed model gave the same ordering (722 site-years at the 43 sites with usable GPP; Fig. X d). A one-standard-deviation wetter year raised the streamflow anomaly by 0.72 SD (95 % CI 0.66–0.77), a warmer year lowered it by 0.10 SD (0.04–0.15), and a greener year lowered it by 0.04 SD (CI −0.09 to 0.01; likelihood-ratio test for adding GPP, p = 0.28). The fixed effects explained 57 % of the variance in the streamflow anomaly, and the estimates were unchanged by adding a site random intercept or by dropping the GPP random slope, whose variance was zero. Adding the previous year's precipitation added a carry-over effect of 0.20 SD (0.15–0.25) and made the GPP coefficient marginally negative (−0.05, CI −0.10 to −0.01). The temperature effect was 0.27 SD per SD stronger at arid than at humid sites (interaction p = 0.004; −0.39 versus −0.07 in separate fits), whereas the GPP effect did not differ between classes (interaction p = 0.87). In proportional terms the precipitation elasticity of streamflow was 1.4 (1.2–1.7) overall, 1.3 at humid and 1.6 at arid sites; streamflow fell by 6 % per °C (1–11 %) overall, by 3 % per °C (0.2–5 %) at humid sites and by 36 % per °C (4–57 %) at the nine arid sites; and the GPP elasticity was −0.20 (−0.51 to 0.10) overall and −0.26 (−0.49 to −0.03) at humid sites, so a year 10 % greener than normal carried at most a few per cent less flow. Other streamflow metrics behaved alike: high flows (95th percentile) followed precipitation as closely as mean flow (median ρ = 0.72), low flows (5th percentile) less so (0.36), the date by which half of the annual flow had passed came earlier in warmer years at every site (median ρ = −0.21, negative at 44 of 44 sites), and GPP, after adjustment for precipitation and temperature, had a median |ρ| of 0.11 or less for every metric (Table SX). At the 13 sites evaluated on June–August flow, summer flow was more weakly tied to water-year precipitation (median ρ = 0.39) and again showed no consistent GPP relation (median ρ = −0.20; p = 0.24).

## Discussion paragraph (Budyko, and why trends are absent)

The interannual sensitivities are what the Budyko framework predicts for a mostly energy-limited set of catchments: streamflow responds to precipitation with an elasticity above one that rises with aridity, temperature matters where evaporative demand exceeds supply (PET/P > 1) and barely at all where it does not, and canopy productivity in a given year changes streamflow by at most a few per cent once precipitation and temperature are accounted for. They also explain the absence of streamflow trends. Combining each site's temperature trend with these sensitivities, the warming observed over the window (median 0.9 °C across the 44 sites; 1.5 °C at the 21 sites with a significant warming trend) implies a median change in mean streamflow of −3 % (−4 % at the significantly warming sites), against a median interannual standard deviation of streamflow of 35 %. No site in this pool had a significant precipitation trend. The nine arid sites are too few, and too variable (interannual standard deviation 148 %), for this calculation to be meaningful there.

## Response-letter note for Stephanie's point

Years with higher than normal GPP are not years with lower than normal streamflow at these sites. Precipitation explains most of the interannual variation in streamflow, temperature explains a second, smaller part that is large only in arid catchments, and once both are accounted for the GPP effect is about −0.04 SD of streamflow per SD of GPP, with a 95 % interval (−0.09 to 0.01) that excludes anything larger than 0.1 SD, and no difference between humid and arid sites; in proportional terms the GPP elasticity is about −0.2, so a year 10 % greener than normal carries at most a few per cent less flow. The same sensitivities, combined with the observed warming, predict a change in mean flow over the window of a few per cent, an order of magnitude below the interannual variability, which is why the trend analysis finds so little. One limitation to state: the within-site approach needs at least six complete years, so it adds only two non-experimental sites to the trend pool, and 41 non-experimental headwater sites with one to five complete years cannot be used by either analysis.

## Caveats and open choices

- Minimum record length is set to 6 years (`min_years_anomaly` in the script); 10 is the trend rule. Lowering it further mainly affects the mixed model, since Spearman correlations on fewer than six pairs are uninformative.
- The main mixed model is flagged singular because the GPP random-slope variance collapses to zero (no detectable site-to-site variation in the GPP effect). Dropping that slope gives a non-singular fit with identical fixed effects; adding a site random intercept also changes nothing.
- The arid class has nine sites and 89 site-years; its temperature elasticity (and therefore the arid "expected change") is poorly constrained.
- GPP and P barely covary within these sites (median within-site ρ = 0.02); GPP and T covary a little more (0.22). The P-only partial correlation at arid sites (−0.27) is partly a temperature signal (−0.21 with T removed as well, one of nine sites significant).
- With water-year GPP the GPP effect is small but borderline across specifications: main z-score model −0.04 SD per SD (LRT p = 0.28); −0.05 (p = 0.025) once prior-year P is included; humid log–log elasticity −0.26 (p = 0.03); partial Spearman median −0.07 (p = 0.03). Report it as "at most a few per cent", not as "no effect" and not as a detected effect.
- Pooled OLS lines in the figure: panel b (Q vs P) R² = 0.59 humid, 0.61 arid; panel c (GPP, P + T removed) R² = 0.00 humid, 0.01 arid (section 6 of the numbers file).
- The expected-change calculation multiplies each site's Sen slope by 22 years and applies the class-specific elasticity coefficients; it is an order-of-magnitude argument, not a prediction.
- Summer-window (JJA) sites are secondary and analysed against water-year P, T and GPP.
- A Budyko-space variant (observed evaporative index minus the Fu-curve expectation versus driver anomalies) could use the 1–5-year sites, because driver anomalies exist for all 23 years at every site; only the streamflow baseline is missing. Not built.

## Methods paragraph (after the anomaly-analysis Methods paragraph)

To include sites with too few complete years for within-site standardization, we repeated the analysis in Budyko space, where the expected evapotranspiration for a single year comes from the water-energy balance rather than from the site's own record. For every site-year with a complete streamflow record we computed the aridity index (Daymet PET over PRISM precipitation, water-year totals) and the observed evaporative index, (P - Q)/P, with Q as the mean daily flow scaled to the water year. The residual from the Fu curve, EI = 1 + AI - (1 + AI^w)^(1/w) with w = 2.6 (Zhang et al 2004), measures how much more (positive) or less (negative) water the catchment lost to evapotranspiration that year than its aridity predicts. We modelled the residual on the GPP and temperature anomalies with random intercepts by site and water year (lme4), for all sites, for sites with at least six complete years, for sites with one to five, and by aridity class, and checked sensitivity to the fitted curve parameter (w = 2.3), to the within-site aridity anomaly as an extra covariate, and to excluding site-years with Q greater than P. Residual effects are expressed as a percentage of mean streamflow at the median runoff ratio of the sites in each fit.

## Results paragraph (after the anomaly Results paragraphs)

Placing each site-year in Budyko space let 41 further non-experimental sites with one to five complete years, 30 of them arid, join the 44 longer-record sites with their 96 site-years, for 85 sites and 836 site-years (Fig. Y a). The sites that trend detection cannot use are therefore mostly dry ones: 9 of the 44 longer-record sites are arid (20 %) against 30 of the 41 short-record sites (73 %). Site-years scattered around the Fu curve with a fitted parameter of 2.3, and 90 % of the residual variance was between sites, 9 % within sites and under 1 % common to a water year. Once each year's aridity had set the expected evapotranspiration, temperature had no further effect on the residual (pooled Spearman rho = 0.00; mixed-model effect −1 % of mean flow per SD, 95 % CI −3 to 1 %), so the temperature sensitivity seen in the anomaly analysis is the one implied by evaporative demand. GPP had a small residual effect: greener years lost 2.3 % more of mean streamflow per SD of GPP anomaly (CI 0.2–4.3 %; p = 0.03). The estimate was the same with the longer-record sites alone (2.1 %), consistent but uninformative from the short-record sites alone (7 %, CI −6 to 20 %), 3.0 % after allowing for prior-year precipitation, and 1.7 % (p = 0.07) after excluding the 13 site-years with streamflow exceeding precipitation. Adding the within-site aridity anomaly as a covariate, which is close to repeating the precipitation-and-temperature adjustment of the anomaly analysis inside Budyko space, moved the GPP effect to 1.3 % (not significant) and the temperature effect to 2.8 % per SD (CI 0.7–5.0 %), so the two approaches agree on the GPP effect only once they condition on the same variables. By aridity class the effect was 1.5 % at humid sites (CI −0.4 to 3.4 %) and 7.8 % at the 38 arid sites (CI 0.1–15.5 %; interaction p = 0.049; Fig. Y b–d). Prior-year precipitation had the clearest residual signal: a wetter previous year reduced the current year's evaporative index by the equivalent of 4.7 % of mean flow per SD (CI 2.6–6.8 %), the carry-over of storage that the single-year water balance omits.

## Discussion sentences (append to the Budyko paragraph)

The Budyko-space version reaches the same conclusion from a baseline that does not depend on record length: with the year's aridity accounted for, temperature has no effect beyond the evaporative demand already in the aridity index, and canopy productivity adds at most a few per cent, with the larger and less certain effect in the water-limited catchments that the trend analysis mostly cannot see. The one clear residual signal is storage carry-over from the previous year's precipitation.

## Caveats specific to the Budyko version

- The residual is dominated by between-site offsets (90 % of its variance): PRISM precipitation bias, groundwater import or export and snow storage move whole sites off the curve. The site random intercept absorbs this, but singleton sites contribute to the GPP and T effects only through shrunken intercepts.
- 13 non-experimental site-years at six sites (EB, ER_AVY1, ER_RUS1, GGU, GREEN4, UpperJaramilloSpring) have Q greater than P, which the water balance cannot produce; they are kept in the main fit and dropped in a sensitivity fit.
- Within sites, the evaporative index responds less to aridity than the Fu curve predicts (residual vs AI anomaly rho = −0.19). Adding the AI anomaly as a covariate absorbs this, but because AI is PET/P it is not an independent curve-shape check: it conditions on the year's precipitation and evaporative demand, much as the anomaly analysis does, and under it GPP is not significant while T becomes significant (2.8 % per SD). Report both coefficients from that fit or neither.
- New citations for the reference manager (in-text only in the draft): Fu, B. P. (1981). On the calculation of the evaporation from land surface. Scientia Atmospherica Sinica, 5, 23–31 (in Chinese). Zhang, L., Hickel, K., Dawes, W. R., Chiew, F. H. S., Western, A. W., & Briggs, P. R. (2004). A rational function approach for estimating mean annual evapotranspiration. Water Resources Research, 40, W02502.
- The GPP effect is borderline (p between 0.03 and 0.2 across specifications) and should be reported as "at most a few per cent", not as a detected effect.
