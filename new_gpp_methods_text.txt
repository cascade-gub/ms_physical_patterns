We replaced Landsat-derived GPP (Robinson et al. 2018) with cross-calibrated Landsat
greenness, because GPP trends were inflated by the 2013 transition from the Landsat 7
Enhanced Thematic Mapper Plus (ETM+) to the Landsat 8 Operational Land Imager (OLI). In each
watershed, we randomly sampled 30 Landsat pixel centers at least 45 m apart (3,764 in total).
At each pixel we extracted Collection 2 Level-2
surface reflectance from every Landsat 5, 7, 8, and 9 scene from 1984 to 2025 using
Google Earth Engine. We then processed the time series with the LandsatTS R package
(v1.2.3; Berner et al. 2023). LandsatTS masked clouds, shadow, snow, water, and
low-quality observations, and computed NDVI and NIRv (Badgley et al. 2017). It
cross-calibrated Landsat 5 and 8 observations to Landsat 7 using random forest models
trained on our own pixels. To define each pixel's growing season, LandsatTS fit a smooth
seasonal curve of greenness against day of year, pooling five years of observations
centered on the year of interest. The growing season was the span of days on which this
curve was at or above 75% of its peak. A pixel's annual value was the median of that
year's observations falling within its growing season.

We modified the standard LandsatTS workflow as follows:
1. added Landsat 9 to the extraction and treated it as Landsat 8; the two agreed to
   within 0.002 NDVI,
2. excluded Landsat 7 observations after 2017, when orbital drift began shifting its
   overpass time, so that drift affected neither calibration nor the series,
3. extracted year-round imagery and widened the calibration window from June–August
   to May–September, to suit temperate growing seasons,
4. shortened the curve-fitting window from 7 to 5 years and lowered its minimum
   from 20 to 10 observations, so that the curve follows change more closely and pixels
   with sparse records (common in the 1980s-90s and in cloudy regions) are retained. We
   also lowered the minimum valid NIRv value from 0.15 to 0.02, because the default is
   set for NDVI and NIRv values are roughly 4-6x smaller.

A pixel needed at least two growing-season observations to receive a value for a given
year. Because clouds determine which pixels have values in any given year, we did not
simply average pixels within a watershed. Instead, a watershed's annual value was its
long-term average greenness plus the median, across its pixels, of each pixel's
departure from its own long-term average that year. Years in which fewer than half of a
watershed's pixels had values were left blank. Before calibration,
Landsat 8 read higher than Landsat 7 by 4.9% (NDVI) and 9.8% (NIRv); after calibration,
the cross-validated bias was −0.2% and +0.3%.

To check the Landsat 8/9 calibration, we compared it with LEOHS linear band harmonization
(Richardson et al. 2025), fitted by ordinary least squares, reduced major axis (RMA), and
Theil-Sen regression to near-simultaneous Landsat 7/8 image pairs over our watersheds, and
with a series built from Landsat 7 alone, without calibration. Our calibration and the
least-squares and Theil-Sen harmonizations matched Landsat 7 to within 0.005 NDVI at the
2013 transition; RMA overcorrected by 0.018.
